Leaderboard Office Methodology
Design notes

Taxes get done at kitchen tables.

Usually in March, by someone tired, next to a pile of paper. The five households in this benchmark are made up. Their problems aren’t: a nurse’s overtime, a widow’s hospital bills, a hairdresser who never filed for 2022. What we build should feel like that table on a good day. Daylight, paper, a plant on the sill, and someone patient helping.

See a rollout as a day The six principles
 
1

What we mean by human-centered

The benchmark asks how much money an agent finds for a family. So the family is the subject, and the agent is the help. Six rules follow from that. Each one comes with a small working example.

People before percentages

We open on the household. A result is money that stays with a family, so the first number you see is dollars and a name. The score comes second, in smaller type. Agents are named in the margin, not the headline.

So we don’t put a model’s name in 120-point type, or rank families by how hard they were.

Daylight, not deadlines

Taxes already come with a deadline. We don’t add another. Time here is daylight: a rollout starts at dawn and the sun moves as the agent spends its budget. An agent that runs over keeps working after dark, which is what overtime looks like. Drag the sun.

So we don’t show clocks, countdowns, red alerts or black screens.

Made by hand, measured exactly

Paper has tooth, paint has edges, glass is frosted. These surfaces say a person made this for a person to read. The texture stays on the edges. A bar painted with a dry brush ends exactly where its number says.

So we don’t let a brush stroke change a length, or a texture hide a label.

Weather says what happened

You should feel how a rollout went before you read a number. Clear sky while the agent finds things. A cloud when it reads something with nothing in it. Rain when it falls for a bad pitch. Each find opens a flower.

So we don’t use weather as decoration. If the sky changes, something happened.

Readable first

Body text at 18px. Headings top out around 46px. Lines run about 65 characters. Interface type is Atkinson Hyperlegible, drawn for the Braille Institute so that readers with low vision can tell I from l from 1. When a sentence can replace a chart, we write the sentence.

So we don’t make a word huge because it looks expensive.

One thing at a time

Show the headline, then let people open the rest: the math behind a number, the source behind the math, the phone call behind the source. Drawers and tabs, so the page is never a wall.

So we don’t put every number on screen at once.
2

A rollout is a day

This is how we want the office to show an agent at work. The day starts when the folder lands. The sun crosses as tokens get spent. Each run gets its own weather, the way days do, and the agent’s work moves through it. By dusk the plant on the sill is the strategy.

4

Showing numbers

Numbers are where a page goes cold. These are the patterns we use to keep them warm without bending them. They share one selection: pick a household or an agent anywhere and the rest follow.

Lead with the household

Dollars and a name first. The share and the agent come after, smaller. Found, part found, missed and wrong all sit on one scale.

The strategy, item by item

    Compare in a table, light up one column

    Seven agents on five households. Click a name to follow that agent through the rest of the page. A green dot marks the best in each row.

    Quote the people

    What a family says on the phone is often the fact that matters most. We print it the way they said it, and say where it came from.

    Cost as daylight

    Tokens are drawn as how much of the day an agent used. Past the dashed line it is working after sunset, on paid time.

    A garden for each agent

    Five pots, one per household. Count the flowers to see what it found and the closed buds to see what it missed. Pick an agent here; click a pot to read its strategy above.

    5

    Rules we keep

    Motion

    • Slow. Most changes take 0.6 to 1.2 seconds and ease out.
    • Ambient loops last 20 seconds or more.
    • Anything that moves has a pause button.
    • Reduced motion turns it all off.

    Color

    • No pure black, anywhere. Evening is indigo.
    • One highlight per view.
    • Brick red only for a claim that’s wrong.
    • Color always means something. The legend says what.

    Type

    • 18px body, 46px largest heading.
    • Figures in Atkinson Mono, tabular.
    • Sentence case everywhere.
    • Small caps only for labels.

    Words

    • Plain words a family would use.
    • Dollars before percentages.
    • Say “simulated” wherever it is.
    • Name people by first name once we’ve met them.
